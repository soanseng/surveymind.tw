

# **The Hypomania Checklist (HCL-32): A Comprehensive Clinical and Psychometric Review**

## **Part 1: The HCL-32: Origins and Instrument Design**

### **Introduction: The Clinical Imperative for Hypomania Screening**

The accurate diagnosis of mood disorders represents a persistent and significant challenge in clinical psychiatry. A central issue within this domain is the frequent under-recognition and misdiagnosis of Bipolar Disorder (BP), which is often erroneously identified as unipolar Major Depressive Disorder (MDD).1 This diagnostic error is particularly common for Bipolar II Disorder (BP-II), where the characteristic hypomanic episodes are less severe and disruptive than full-blown mania, and may even be perceived by the patient as periods of high productivity or enhanced well-being.3 Patients typically seek treatment during depressive phases, often failing to report past hypomanic periods, which they may not recognize as part of a pathological process.  
The consequences of this misdiagnosis are severe. The standard treatment for MDD often involves antidepressant monotherapy, which, when administered to a patient with an underlying bipolar diathesis, can precipitate iatrogenic (hypo)mania, induce rapid cycling between mood states, or otherwise destabilize the course of the illness.1 Such inappropriate or delayed treatment is associated with a worsened prognosis, increased functional impairment, and a heightened risk of suicide.4 Studies have shown that patients with BP-II may endure nearly 12 years of misdiagnosis before receiving appropriate treatment with mood stabilizers.4 This substantial diagnostic lag underscores a critical gap in clinical practice: the need for a systematic and sensitive method to screen for a lifetime history of hypomanic symptoms in patients presenting with depression. It is this clinical imperative that directly led to the development of specialized screening instruments designed to look beyond the presenting depressive episode and uncover the tell-tale signs of bipolarity.

### **Development by Dr. Jules Angst and Rationale**

To address the widespread under-detection of bipolar spectrum conditions, the 32-item Hypomania Checklist (HCL-32) was developed by a team led by the renowned Swiss psychiatrist Dr. Jules Angst.3 The primary and explicit goal of the HCL-32 is to serve as a sensitive screening instrument to identify hypomanic features and past hypomanic episodes in patients with a primary complaint of depression.8 By systematically probing for the characteristic symptoms of hypomania, the questionnaire aims to help clinicians in diverse settings—from primary care to specialized psychiatric practice—to consider and correctly diagnose BP-II and other Bipolar Spectrum Disorders (BSD).3  
The instrument was conceived not as a tool for diagnosing personality factors or quantifying the severity of a current mood state, but rather to capture the episodic nature of hypomania and the specific cognitive, affective, and behavioral changes associated with these "high" periods.3 Its development was a direct response to the limitations of existing measures and the urgent need for a tool that could efficiently and reliably flag patients who require a more thorough diagnostic evaluation for bipolarity. The HCL-32 is therefore positioned as an auxiliary tool to enhance clinical suspicion and guide the diagnostic process toward a more accurate conclusion, thereby reducing the profound personal and public health costs of misdiagnosed bipolar disorder.

### **Design Philosophy: Accessibility and Focus**

The design philosophy of the HCL-32 is rooted in clinical pragmatism and accessibility. Recognizing that the initial point of contact for many patients with mood disorders is a general medical setting rather than a psychiatric specialist, the questionnaire was intentionally crafted to be user-friendly.3 It employs short, simple phrases and avoids complex psychiatric jargon, making it easy for individuals from various educational backgrounds to read and complete without extensive instruction.3  
The HCL-32 is a self-administered, self-report questionnaire, which allows for efficient data collection in busy clinical environments.6 This approach empowers patients to reflect on their own history of mood fluctuations. Importantly, the University of Zurich, which holds the copyright, has made the HCL-32 available for clinical and research use at no charge, further promoting its widespread adoption and application in an international context.3 This commitment to open access ensures that the barrier to screening for this critical condition is as low as possible for clinicians and healthcare systems worldwide.

## **Part 2: The Original HCL-32 Questionnaire: Formal Structure and Items**

### **Structure and Administration**

The formal structure of the HCL-32 is more comprehensive than a simple 32-item list. Its administration begins with a crucial introductory section designed to frame the respondent's task correctly. This preamble explains that everyone experiences mood swings and clarifies that the questionnaire's aim is to assess the specific characteristics of their personal "high" periods.12 This framing is essential as it orients the respondent to retrospectively access memories of distinct episodes of elevated mood, energy, and activity, rather than simply describing their general disposition or current feelings. The instructions explicitly ask the respondent to recall these periods while not under the influence of drugs or alcohol to isolate endogenous mood shifts.12

### **Preliminary Assessment Questions**

Before proceeding to the main symptom checklist, the HCL-32 includes two preliminary questions that provide vital context for interpreting the subsequent responses. These questions help to situate the patient's self-reported "highs" relative to both their current state and their baseline temperament.

1. **Current Mental State:** The first question asks the respondent to rate their current state on a 7-point scale ranging from "Much worse than usual" to "Much better than usual".6 This item serves to capture the respondent's mood at the time of completing the questionnaire, which can be a relevant variable in the clinical interpretation of their retrospective report.  
2. **Typical Disposition:** The second question asks the respondent to compare their typical level of activity, energy, and mood to that of other people, choosing from options such as "always rather stable and even," "generally higher," "generally lower," or "repeatedly shows periods of ups and downs".12 This question provides insight into the respondent's perception of their own temperament and the chronicity of their mood fluctuations, helping to distinguish trait-like characteristics from the state-dependent symptoms of a mood episode.

### **The 32-Item Symptom Checklist**

The core of the instrument is the checklist of 32 potential symptoms of hypomania. The respondent is instructed to consider a time when they were in a "high" state and answer "Yes" or "No" to each item based on whether it described their experience during that period. The full, formal questionnaire, including all introductory and supplementary components, is presented in Table 2.1. The supplementary questions, which probe the functional impact of the "highs," others' reactions, and the duration and frequency of episodes, are often included in the full clinical version of the tool and are critical for a comprehensive assessment.12

## **Part 3: Psychometric Properties and Evaluation Protocol**

### **3.1. Scoring Methodology**

The scoring protocol for the HCL-32 is straightforward and designed for ease of use in clinical practice. The core of the score is derived from Part 2, the 32-item symptom checklist. Each item is presented in a dichotomous "Yes" or "No" format.6 The total HCL-32 score is calculated by summing the number of "Yes" responses. For each "Yes," one point is given, and for each "No," zero points are given. This yields a total score that can range from 0 to 32\.14 A higher total score reflects a greater number of endorsed hypomanic symptoms experienced during a "high" period, suggesting a higher likelihood of a bipolar spectrum condition.14 The preliminary and impact questions (Parts 1 and 3\) are not included in this numerical total but provide crucial qualitative and contextual data for clinical interpretation.

### **3.2. The Cutoff Score: A Synthesis of International Validation Studies**

A critical aspect of any screening tool is the establishment of a cutoff score that optimally balances sensitivity (the ability to correctly identify true cases) and specificity (the ability to correctly identify non-cases). While a standard cutoff for the HCL-32 is often cited, a comprehensive review of the literature reveals a more complex and context-dependent picture.  
The most frequently referenced optimal cutoff score for distinguishing Bipolar Disorder (BP) from Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is **14**.14 This threshold was established in the original European validation study, where it yielded a high sensitivity of 0.80 but a modest specificity of 0.51.5 This finding has been replicated in several subsequent international studies. For example, a key validation study in Taiwan found that a cutoff of 14 produced a sensitivity of 0.82 and a specificity of 0.67 for discriminating between BP and MDD.1 Similarly, studies in mainland China have also supported a cutoff of 14, though with varying psychometric properties, in one case showing a sensitivity of 0.69 and a much higher specificity of 0.98.5  
However, a deeper analysis of the research reveals that a single, universal cutoff score of 14 is an oversimplification. The optimal threshold for the HCL-32 appears to be highly dependent on the specific clinical population being assessed and the diagnostic question being asked. For instance, a large study in China found that while 14 was optimal for BP vs. MDD overall, a lower cutoff of **13** was better suited for the specific task of distinguishing BP-II from MDD, yielding a sensitivity of 0.77 and a specificity of 0.62.7 This suggests that a slightly lower threshold may be needed to capture the often more subtle presentation of BP-II.  
This variability is further highlighted by other research. A smaller Taiwanese study that focused specifically on patients already receiving medication for depression found a dramatically lower optimal cutoff of **7/8**, which achieved a perfect sensitivity of 100% but a low specificity of 46.2%.17 This discrepancy illustrates how factors like medication status and sample size can profoundly influence psychometric findings. Furthermore, recent innovative research has demonstrated that personality traits can significantly impact HCL-32 scores. One study found that the optimal cutoff for identifying BP was 15 in patients with typical extraverted personalities but only 12.5 in those with atypical extraversion (introversion).5  
The clear implication from this body of evidence is that the HCL-32 cutoff score is not an absolute, fixed value. Clinicians cannot blindly apply a score of 14 without considering the context. The choice of cutoff represents a trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, and the optimal balance depends on the clinical goal. A lower cutoff (e.g., 13 or even lower) might be appropriate in a setting where maximizing detection (high sensitivity) is paramount and a higher rate of false positives can be managed through follow-up interviews. Conversely, a higher cutoff might be used when trying to minimize false positives. The generally modest specificity reported in many large-scale studies underscores a critical point: the HCL-32 is a screening tool, and a positive score must always be followed by a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation to avoid mislabeling patients.

### **3.3. Internal Structure: Factorial Validity of the HCL-32**

Beyond the total score, understanding the underlying dimensions or constructs measured by the HCL-32 is crucial for its interpretation. This is achieved through factor analysis, a statistical technique that groups related items together. Across numerous validation studies in different languages and cultures, a remarkably consistent factor structure has emerged, providing strong evidence for the instrument's construct validity.  
The most commonly identified internal structure is a **two-factor model**.2 This model conceptualizes hypomania as having two distinct but related dimensions, often referred to as the "bright" and "dark" sides:

* **Factor 1: "Active/Elated" Hypomania:** This factor represents the "sunny" or euphoric aspect of hypomania. It consistently includes items related to increased energy, elevated mood, heightened self-confidence, greater sociability, increased creativity, and faster thinking (e.g., "I feel more energetic," "I am more self-confident," "I have more ideas," "My mood is higher, more optimistic").2  
* **Factor 2: "Risk-Taking/Irritable" Hypomania:** This factor captures the more problematic and dysphoric elements of hypomania. It groups items related to impulsivity, risky behaviors, irritability, and interpersonal conflict (e.g., "I spend more money," "I get into more quarrels," "I can be exhausting or irritating for others," "I take more risks").8

Several studies, particularly those conducted in Asian populations, have refined this model further, identifying a **three-factor structure**.6 In this model, the "Active/Elated" factor remains largely intact, but the "Risk-Taking/Irritable" factor is split into two separate components:

* **Factor 2: "Risk-Taking":** This factor specifically isolates behaviors involving impulsivity and potential danger, such as spending too much money, driving faster, taking more risks in daily life, and increased substance use.6  
* **Factor 3: "Irritable":** This factor captures the mood lability and interpersonal friction associated with some hypomanic states, including items like being more impatient, getting into more quarrels, and being perceived as irritating by others.6

The consistent emergence of these two- and three-factor solutions across diverse samples—including European, Chinese, and Korean populations—is a powerful testament to the HCL-32's robust construct validity.2 It suggests that these dimensions represent fundamental and cross-culturally recognizable components of the hypomanic experience. This factorial structure is not merely a statistical artifact; it has direct clinical utility. Analyzing a patient's scores on these subscales can provide a more nuanced clinical portrait than the total score alone. For example, a patient with a high score on the "Risk-Taking/Irritable" factor may present a greater immediate risk to themselves or their relationships, warranting more urgent intervention, even if their total HCL-32 score is only moderately elevated. Some research suggests that this subscore may even offer better specificity in discriminating between clinical groups than the full scale.2

### **3.4. Reliability and Clinical Limitations**

A psychometrically sound instrument must be both reliable (consistent) and used with a clear understanding of its limitations. The HCL-32 has demonstrated strong reliability across numerous studies. Internal consistency, a measure of how well the items on a scale measure the same underlying construct, is consistently high. Cronbach's alpha coefficients for the total scale are typically excellent, reported as 0.82 in the original development study, 0.88 in a large Chinese sample, 0.94 in a Spanish validation, and 0.86 in a Portuguese version.2 The instrument has also shown good temporal stability, with high test-retest reliability coefficients indicating that scores remain stable over short periods in the absence of clinical change.11  
Despite its strengths, the responsible use of the HCL-32 requires a keen awareness of its inherent limitations:

* **A Screening Tool, Not a Diagnostic Instrument:** This is the most critical limitation to emphasize. A high score on the HCL-32 is suggestive of bipolarity, but it does not constitute a formal diagnosis.2 A positive screen must always be followed by a comprehensive clinical evaluation, preferably using a structured diagnostic interview such as the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders (SCID) or the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia-Lifetime (SADS-L), to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other conditions.1  
* **Inability to Differentiate Bipolar Subtypes:** The HCL-32 was designed to screen for a history of hypomanic symptoms broadly and is generally unable to reliably distinguish between Bipolar I Disorder (characterized by at least one manic episode) and Bipolar II Disorder (characterized by hypomanic and major depressive episodes) based on the total score.2 While one Taiwanese study proposed a very high cutoff score of 21 might help differentiate the two, this finding is not widely replicated and the tool's primary utility remains in identifying potential bipolarity in general.1  
* **Susceptibility to Self-Report Bias:** Like all self-report inventories, HCL-32 scores can be influenced by the respondent's insight, memory, and willingness to report symptoms accurately. Scores can be consciously or unconsciously exaggerated or minimized, which can affect the validity of the screening result.3  
* **Influence of Current Mood State:** The HCL-32 is a retrospective measure, asking about past "highs." While some research suggests that the respondent's current mood state does not have a significant effect on their total score 15, other studies have found a small but statistically significant positive correlation between a more elevated current mood and a higher HCL-32 score.16 This suggests that a person's current affective state (either depressed or elevated) may color their recall of past episodes, introducing a potential source of bias.

## **Part 4: The HCL-32 in a Chinese Context: Translation and Regional Validation**

### **4.1. Cross-Cultural Validation and Adaptation**

The global utility of the HCL-32 has been established through its translation and validation in numerous languages and cultures. The process for creating the Chinese versions of the HCL-32 involved rigorous methodology to ensure its cross-cultural validity. Studies validating the instrument in both mainland China and Taiwan report using a formal translation and back-translation process.7 This standard psychometric procedure involves translating the original English instrument into the target language (Chinese), and then having an independent, bilingual expert translate the new version back into English. This back-translated version is then compared to the original to ensure that the meaning, nuance, and intent of each item have been preserved. This meticulous process helps to guarantee the linguistic and conceptual equivalence of the translated scale.16  
A crucial finding from this body of research is the explicit distinction made between the version of the HCL-32 used in mainland China and the version used in Taiwan.7 The differences are not merely a matter of using Simplified Chinese characters (简体字) in the mainland versus Traditional Chinese characters (繁體字) in Taiwan. The research notes that there are also substantive differences in terminology, particularly in the words used to express concepts related to "anxiety and emotion".7  
This linguistic and cultural nuance is of paramount importance. It means that simply converting a Simplified Chinese version of the questionnaire into Traditional characters would be insufficient and psychometrically unsound for use in a Taiwanese population. The validity of a psychological instrument hinges on its ability to communicate concepts in a way that is natural and accurately understood by the target population. Therefore, a properly adapted Traditional Chinese version (zh-tw) is necessary for reliable screening in Taiwan.

### **4.3. Validation Findings from Taiwan**

The psychometric properties of the Traditional Chinese HCL-32 have been established through validation studies conducted in Taiwan. These studies provide the necessary evidence base for its use in that specific clinical and cultural context.  
The principal validation study, conducted at the National Cheng Kung University, established the Taiwanese version of the HCL-32 as a reliable and valid screening tool.1 The key findings from this research indicated that the instrument possessed high internal consistency and confirmed the dual-factor structure ("active/elated" and "risk-taking/irritable") that has been found in many other international studies, supporting its construct validity within the Taiwanese population.1 For its primary purpose of discriminating between Bipolar Disorder (BP) and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), the study identified an optimal cutoff score of  
**14**. At this threshold, the scale demonstrated good discriminatory power, yielding a **sensitivity of 82%** and a **specificity of 67%**.1  
However, the psychometric landscape in Taiwan is not entirely uniform. A different and smaller study conducted in Taiwan produced a notably divergent finding.17 This study, which specifically recruited 59 participants who were already medicated for an initial diagnosis of unipolar depression, found a much lower optimal cutoff point of  
**7/8**. At this lower threshold, the HCL-32 achieved a perfect sensitivity of 100% (identifying all true bipolar cases within the sample) but at the cost of a very low specificity of 46.2% (meaning it incorrectly flagged more than half of the true unipolar depression patients as potentially bipolar).  
The stark contrast between a cutoff of 14 in the larger validation study and a cutoff of 7/8 in this smaller, more specific sample provides a powerful real-world illustration of how sample characteristics can dramatically influence a screening tool's performance. The lower cutoff in the medicated sample might be explained in several ways. The psychotropic medications may have blunted the full expression of hypomanic symptoms, making a lower threshold necessary for detection. Alternatively, the small sample size may have produced a statistically anomalous result that is not generalizable to the broader population. This discrepancy reinforces the critical conclusion that cutoff scores are not absolute. They are statistical estimations derived from specific samples, and clinicians must consider the characteristics of their own patients (e.g., medication status, severity of illness) when interpreting an HCL-32 score.

## **Part 5: Synthesis and Recommendations for Clinical Application**

### **Summary of Key Findings**

This comprehensive review establishes the Hypomania Checklist-32 (HCL-32) as a psychometrically robust, accessible, and valuable instrument for the screening of bipolar spectrum disorders. Developed to address the critical clinical problem of misdiagnosed bipolarity, its utility is supported by extensive international research. The evidence demonstrates that the HCL-32 is a reliable measure with high internal consistency across multiple languages and cultures. Its validity is strongly supported by a consistent underlying factor structure, typically comprising two key dimensions: an "Active/Elated" factor and a "Risk-Taking/Irritable" factor. This structure appears to represent fundamental, cross-culturally relevant aspects of the hypomanic experience. While a cutoff score of 14 is frequently cited as optimal for distinguishing Bipolar Disorder from Major Depressive Disorder, the evidence clearly shows that this threshold is variable and highly dependent on the specific clinical population, diagnostic goal, and even individual patient characteristics like personality.

### **Emphasis on Integrated Clinical Judgment**

The single most important conclusion from this analysis is that the HCL-32 is a powerful adjunct to, but not a substitute for, skilled clinical judgment and a thorough diagnostic assessment. Its primary strength lies in its sensitivity—its ability to cast a wide net and identify individuals who might otherwise be missed. However, this sensitivity often comes at the cost of modest specificity, creating a risk of false positives. Therefore, a score on the HCL-32, regardless of whether it is above or below a given cutoff, should never be the sole basis for a diagnosis. It should be seen as a piece of data that prompts further clinical inquiry. The instrument's value is maximized when it is used to structure a conversation with the patient about their history of mood fluctuations, providing a starting point for a deeper exploration of their experiences.

### **Recommendations for Use**

Based on the extensive body of research reviewed, the following recommendations are provided for the responsible and effective application of the HCL-32 in clinical and research settings:

1. **Integrate, Do Not Isolate:** Always use the HCL-32 as one component of a comprehensive evaluation. A positive screen should automatically trigger a more in-depth assessment, including a detailed longitudinal history of mood symptoms and functional impairment, and ideally, a structured diagnostic interview.  
2. **Contextualize the Cutoff Score:** Clinicians must be flexible and thoughtful when considering a cutoff score. A score of 14 serves as a useful general guideline, but the clinical context is paramount. Factors such as the patient's current medication status, personality profile, the severity of their presenting symptoms, and the clinical setting should all inform the interpretation of the total score.  
3. **Utilize the Factor Sub-scores:** To gain a more nuanced understanding of a patient's symptom profile, clinicians should look beyond the total score and consider the pattern of endorsed items. Analyzing responses based on the "Active/Elated" and "Risk-Taking/Irritable" factors can provide valuable insights into the specific nature of the patient's "highs" and help tailor risk assessment and treatment planning.  
4. **Ensure Cultural and Linguistic Appropriateness:** When screening individuals from diverse backgrounds, it is imperative to use a version of the HCL-32 that has been properly translated and validated for that specific population. As demonstrated by the differences between the Simplified Chinese version for mainland China and the Traditional Chinese version for Taiwan, simple script conversion is insufficient. Using a culturally and linguistically appropriate instrument is fundamental to achieving valid and reliable results.
